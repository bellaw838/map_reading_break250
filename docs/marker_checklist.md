# The Advanced Reader's Marker Checklist

A one-page inventory of everything an advanced reader notices and marks while reading a passage. Use this as a legend when marking up real articles, novels, or test passages — with a real highlighter or a digital one.

---

## How to use this

When reading a passage carefully:

1. **First pass — read for understanding.** Don't mark anything yet.
2. **Second pass — mark with purpose.** For each move you spot, use the color/label below.
3. **Stop after each paragraph.** Ask: *what JOB did that paragraph do?* (See Structure.)
4. **At the end, look at your markup.** It should show the text's argument, attitude, and architecture at a glance.

If your markup is sparse, you're under-reading. If everything's marked, you're over-marking — be selective.

---

## I. The five color-coded categories

These are what Reading Lab uses. They cover the most-tested moves on MAP-style reading.

Think of them as five big questions:

| Color | Code | Big question | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| 🟡 Amber | `TO` | **How does it feel?** | Tone & Word Choice |
| 🟢 Green | `E` | **How do I prove it?** | Evidence |
| 🔵 Blue | `TH` | **What is it really saying?** | Theme / Central Idea |
| 🟣 Purple | `INF` | **What is suggested but not said?** | Inference Clues |
| 🟠 Orange | `STR` | **How is it built?** | Structure / Function |

The color tells you the family. The note tells you the exact move.

### 🟡 HOW DOES IT FEEL? — TONE & WORD CHOICE (`TO`)

Things to mark — anything that reveals the author's or speaker's **attitude**:

- **Hedging verbs** that signal doubt: *claimed, supposedly, alleged, asserted, reportedly*
- **Strong verbs** that signal certainty: *proves, demonstrates, shows, refutes, establishes*
- **Contrast words** that signal a turn: *although, however, despite, yet, but, while*
- **Distancing phrases**: *"so-called," "what they call," "if true," "according to"*
- **Loaded / charged vocabulary** — emotionally weighted words: *outrage, scheme, triumph, disaster*
- **Tone words** the author uses or that you'd use to name the tone: *skeptical, nostalgic, dismissive, urgent, reflective, wry*
- **Tone shifts** — places where the attitude changes mid-passage
- **Mood vs Tone** — note when the reader's feeling diverges from the author's attitude (a horror story narrated calmly)
- **Irony / sarcasm** — language meant the opposite of literally
- **Register** — formal ("indeed," "moreover") vs casual ("yeah," "stuff") and where it shifts

### 🟢 HOW DO I PROVE IT? — EVIDENCE (`E`)

Things to mark — the **specific words** that prove or support a claim:

- **Direct quotes** vs paraphrases (mark the actual quoted words)
- **Specific facts**, numbers, statistics, dates
- **Attribution phrases**: *"according to," "the study found," "X reports"*
- **Specific examples** used as proof (the Vermont case, the 200-person sample)
- **Best-supported phrases** — the strongest piece of evidence, not just *any* on-topic detail
- **Source signals** — who is saying this, and is the source named?
- **What the evidence DOESN'T prove** — gaps between the data and the claim

### 🔵 WHAT IS IT REALLY SAYING? — THEME / CENTRAL IDEA (`TH`)

Things to mark — the **point** the text is making, not just the topic:

- **Thesis statement** — usually in the first or last paragraph of an informational piece
- **Topic sentences** — the main idea of each paragraph (often the first sentence)
- **Recurring ideas / motifs** — phrases that come back, often signaling the theme
- **Universal claims about life, people, or the world** — theme in narratives lives here
- **Title-related phrases** — the title often echoes the theme
- **Closing claims** — the argument or theme often lands explicitly at the end
- **Theme test:** can it be written as a COMPLETE SENTENCE? "Friendship" is a topic; "true friendship requires honesty" is a theme.

### 🟣 WHAT IS SUGGESTED BUT NOT SAID? — INFERENCE CLUES (`INF`)

Things to mark — details that **suggest** something without saying it:

- **Character actions that reveal feeling** — what a character DOES, especially repeatedly
- **Mismatched actions** — laughing at sad news, smiling when angry (hidden feeling)
- **What characters DON'T say or do** — omissions are clues too
- **Setting details** that hint at mood — empty rooms, weather, lighting
- **Reactions by other characters** — how does someone else respond?
- **Word-choice clues** — connotation that signals attitude or feeling
- **Behavioral patterns** — a character who keeps checking the door (waiting / anxious)
- **The "I-must-conclude" prompts** — when a test asks "the passage suggests" or "it can be inferred"

### 🟠 HOW IS IT BUILT? — STRUCTURE / FUNCTION (`STR`)

Things to mark — how the text is **built**:

- **Signal words**: *because, however, first, although, in contrast, on the other hand, therefore, finally, similarly*
- **Paragraph function**: what JOB does each paragraph do? Options:
  - introduces a topic
  - provides background
  - defines a term
  - gives an example
  - provides evidence
  - shows contrast
  - presents a counterargument
  - refutes
  - explains cause / effect
  - concludes
- **Structural moves**: turn, pivot, volta — the *single word* where the argument changes (e.g., "But" in line 9 of Sonnet 18)
- **Pattern-then-turn**: when the text sets up a repeating pattern and then breaks it (fables, surprise endings)
- **Parallel construction**: "of the people, by the people, for the people"
- **Anaphora**: repeated openings (*"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times…"*)
- **Openers and closers**: the very first and last sentence usually carry weight

## II. Additional moves worth noticing (no color in v1)

These are real moves an advanced reader makes — Reading Lab doesn't color-code them yet, but you should still notice them when marking real texts.

### Figurative language

- **Simile** — comparison with *like* or *as*
- **Metaphor** — direct equation (life = a walking shadow)
- **Personification** — non-human given human traits
- **Imagery** — sensory detail (smell, sound, touch, color)
- **Symbolism** — concrete object standing for an abstract idea
- **Hyperbole** — deliberate exaggeration
- **Understatement** — deliberate downplay
- **Allusion** — reference to another text or event

### Point of view & narrator

- **Narrator vs Author** — the narrator is a voice IN the work; the author is outside it
- **First-person ("I") vs third-person** — what's the access?
- **Reliable vs unreliable narrator** — does the narrator have reasons to mislead?
- **Bias / slant** — even nonfiction has a perspective

### Argument structure

- **Claim** — what's being argued
- **Evidence** — what supports the claim (also marked green above)
- **Reasoning** — how the evidence connects to the claim
- **Assumption** — the unstated belief that lets the evidence "count"
- **Counterclaim** — the opposing view
- **Rebuttal** — the response to the counterclaim
- **Logical moves** — emotional appeal vs logical evidence; bandwagon; false binary

### Vocabulary in context

- **Unfamiliar words** worth decoding from context
- **Near-synonyms** the author chose carefully (*suggests* vs *proves*)
- **Academic verbs** that name the author's move (*assert, imply, concede, scrutinize, undermine, refute*)
- **Connotation** — positive / negative / neutral feeling carried by the word
- **Figurative word uses** — *undermine* an argument, *evoke* a memory

## III. The marker's questions

After each paragraph, ask:

1. **What is this paragraph DOING?** (Introduce, define, give an example, refute, conclude — see Structure.)
2. **What attitude does the author show here?** (Look at word choice.)
3. **What's the central claim this paragraph is making?** (Topic sentence.)
4. **What proves it?** (Evidence — the specific words.)
5. **What's hinted but not said?** (Inference clues.)
6. **What's the turn?** (Find the BUT, HOWEVER, YET, NOW.)

After the whole text:

7. **What's the main idea / theme?** (One complete sentence.)
8. **What's the author's overall tone?** (Name it precisely — not just "negative.")
9. **What's the strongest evidence?** (The single best-supported phrase.)
10. **What did the author NOT say?** (Sometimes the most important move.)

---

## IV. How to practice

1. Pick a short real article (newspaper op-ed, short story, primary source, poem).
2. Read it once for understanding — no marks.
3. Mark it using this checklist (highlighter colors or labels).
4. Write three sentences:
   - **Main idea / theme** of the whole text (complete sentence).
   - **Author's tone** (specific tone word).
   - **Strongest evidence** (specific phrase).
5. Compare your markup with a friend's or teacher's. The disagreements are where the learning is.

Do this once a week. The habit makes the analytical moves automatic.

---

## V. The minimum viable marker kit

If you only had four highlighter colors and one pen, use:

- **Yellow** for **TONE WORDS** — the single most-trapped move at the Break-250 level.
- **Green** for **EVIDENCE** — direct quotes / facts that prove a claim.
- **Blue** for **THEME / MAIN IDEA** — the article's central claim, usually in the first or last paragraph.
- **Pink/purple** for **INFERENCE CLUES** — anything you concluded but the text didn't directly say.
- **Pen** for **STRUCTURE NOTES in the margin** — "intro," "example," "counterargument," "conclusion," "turn."

That's the working kit. Everything else above is enrichment.
