# Blind Review — 67 Break 250 Reading Questions

## Method

I answered each item without using an answer key. For every question, I selected the best-supported answer, quoted the exact wording that supports it, identified the closest alternative, and judged whether the item has one clearly best answer.

**Verdict labels**

- **Clear** — one answer is clearly best and the distractors are fair enough.
- **Revise** — one answer is probably best, but wording, evidence, or a distractor should be improved.
- **Ambiguous / invalid** — two or more answers are defensible, or the stem asks for more certainty than the passage permits.

## Answer sequence

**1–10:** A, A, B, B, C, D, D, C, C, C  
**11–20:** A, B, A, A, B, B, C, A, A, B  
**21–30:** B, A, D, A, C, B, B, A, B, A  
**31–40:** B, A, B, C, D, D, B, A, D, B  
**41–50:** B, B, D, A, D, C, A, B, B, A  
**51–60:** B, C, D, D, C, B, C, B, B, C  
**61–67:** B, C, B, B, C, C, B

## Item-by-item review

### 1. A — Wistful

- **Best support:** “the unused train ticket” and “the journey he never made.” The yearly act of imagining an unrealized journey suggests longing mixed with regret.
- **Second-best:** **C — Detached.** It loses because “every autumn” and “imagines” show continuing emotional attachment, not distance.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 2. A — Ask what feeling or motive best fits the action in context

- **Best support:** The key phrase is “this action **in context**.” An inference should connect behavior to the specific situation.
- **Second-best:** **D — What feeling usually follows this action in stories?** General story knowledge can suggest possibilities, but it cannot replace evidence from this particular scene.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 3. B — Printed items made for brief use or interest

- **Best support:** The examples are “ticket stubs, postcards, and old programs.” These are printed objects commonly made for temporary use or short-term interest.
- **Second-best:** **A — Rare antiques that collectors consider highly valuable.** The objects may be old or collectible, but the sentence never says they are rare or valuable.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 4. B — A single scene’s lesson has been called the theme

- **Best support:** The story has “several important moments,” while the proposed theme fits “only ONE of them.”
- **Second-best:** **C — The theme is too broad.** A broad theme can fail to fit details, but fitting only one moment more directly suggests that the reader generalized from one scene.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 5. C — Announced

- **Best support:** Of the four choices, “announced” most simply reports that someone made information public. “Allegedly” and “supposedly” explicitly distance the writer from the claim; “claimed” can also suggest doubt.
- **Second-best:** **B — Claimed.** It can be neutral in some journalistic contexts, but by itself it is more likely than “announced” to imply that the statement has not been verified.
- **Verdict:** **Clear enough, but this is a single-word vocabulary item rather than a context-reading item.**

### 6. D — Figuratively, she sparked a debate

- **Best support:** “Ignited a debate” cannot sensibly mean setting the debate on fire. “Sparked” preserves the figurative idea of causing the debate to begin.
- **Second-best:** **A — She literally started a small fire.** It follows the literal meaning of “ignited,” but it does not fit the object “a debate.”
- **Verdict:** **Answer is clear, but B and C are unserious distractors for advanced readers. Revise the distractors.**

### 7. D — Sleep is linked to better outcomes, prompting later start times

- **Best support:** The passage combines better “test” scores, fewer missed days, better moods, and the recommendation that “schools … start later.”
- **Second-best:** **C — Student sleep habits are connected to performance in school.** It captures the central relationship but omits the recommendation and does not fully cover mood and attendance.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 8. C — Bitter

- **Best support:** “Old, twisted” is harsh description, and “someone who never reached for me” expresses hurt and resentment over emotional distance.
- **Second-best:** **D — Pitying.** The description of the grandmother’s hands could evoke pity, but “never reached for me” shifts the attitude toward grievance rather than compassion.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 9. C — No, because reluctant allows hesitation before agreeing

- **Best support:** “Reluctant” is followed by “but she eventually agreed.” Refusal would conflict with the eventual agreement unless the refusal later changed.
- **Second-best:** **A — Yes, because both show she preferred to stay.** Both can involve not wanting to leave, but “refused” states a stronger action than hesitation.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 10. C — Patient support can help someone keep going through failure

- **Best support:** The brother “struggles and fails several times,” while the sister “returns and practices with him again,” and eventually he succeeds at all three skills.
- **Second-best:** **A — Good teachers never become frustrated.** The sister’s persistence supports patience, but the story never says she is never frustrated; “never” makes the claim too absolute.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 11. A — Small kindnesses shape how children see themselves

- **Best support:** It is a complete, general claim about human experience rather than a topic, plot fact, or setting detail.
- **Second-best:** **B — The role of school in children’s lives.** This is a topic phrase, not a message about that topic.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 12. B — Find a theme supported across several scenes

- **Second-best:** **C — Add detail to the theme.** More specific wording might improve a vague theme, but it cannot repair the lack of support across the text.
- **Verdict:** **Clear.**

### 13. A — Dad is less certain than his confident words claim

- **Second-best:** **D — Dad is trying to keep someone else from worrying.** His statement could be reassuring, but the passage does not establish another person’s worry or Dad’s motive.

### 14. A — Something kept Tom away from school

### 15. B — Maya realizes the laugh was hurtful and feels sorry

### 16. B — The author praises the gain but presents it as comparatively modest

# Separate validity flags

## A. Items where two answers are equally or nearly equally defensible

### Definite problems

1. **Item 24 — A, C, and D are not cleanly separated.**
   - A is an inference.
   - C adds “on the desk,” which the passage never states.
   - D adds “while she waited,” also not directly stated.
   - This item should not be scored until the distractors are rewritten.

2. **Item 27 — B is the largest unsupported leap, but A and C are also unsupported explanatory inferences.**
   - The stem should ask for the **largest** over-inference, or A and C should be rewritten as clearly supported/clearly stated alternatives.

3. **Item 45 — A and D are both strong clues.**
   - A shows he needed to process the letter more than once.
   - D suggests he will keep engaging with it.
   - The current “best” distinction is too fine for a single-answer item.

### Near ambiguities

4. **Item 23 — A and D are both plausible.**
   - “Didn’t look up from her phone” can indicate emotional concealment, but it can also simply indicate that she does not want to talk.

5. **Item 26 — B is likely intended, but A and even C are possible readings.**
   - “After just two months” may sound skeptical about premature judgment, neutral about timing, or admiring of speed.

## B. Unclear wording or unfair distractors

- **Item 6:** B and C are unserious distractors for advanced readers.
- **Item 24:** Two distractors contain unstated additions, undermining the stated-fact/inference distinction.
- **Item 27:** More than one choice qualifies as unsupported inference.
- **Item 35:** “Quoted evidence” is inaccurate without an actual quoted passage.
- **Item 37:** “First” presents a curriculum strategy as universally necessary; acceptable inside the course, but not universally true.
- **Item 40:** The stem asks for narrator tone while the clues directly describe a character’s delivery.
- **Item 45:** The two strongest clues are too close.
- **Item 65:** “Proves” demands stronger causal evidence than the passage supplies.

## C. Places where the best answer claims more than the passage proves

- **Item 19:** “Strongly affected” is stronger than “the absence is especially salient or important.”
- **Item 23:** Not looking up from a phone alone does not securely establish that she is “not fine.”
- **Item 26:** Skepticism is inferred largely from “just” and is not uniquely established.
- **Item 40:** “Measured” clearly describes the character’s delivery, but not necessarily the narrator’s attitude.
- **Item 45:** Pocketing the letter suggests continued significance, but not conclusively unfinished thinking.
- **Item 65:** The comparison strongly supports tutoring, but does not strictly “prove” causation without random assignment or stronger controls.

## D. Narrator, speaker, author, or character mix-ups

- **Item 40:** The evidence—“spoke evenly,” “without raising his voice,” and “each word landed with care”—most directly describes the **character’s manner**, not the **narrator’s tone**. Rewrite the stem or add narrator evaluation.
- **Item 43, minor caution:** “A small private smile” is a character detail that can help create tone, but students should not be taught that character emotion and narrator attitude are automatically the same. This item remains answerable as written.

## E. Items whose answer depends mainly on a single word or fixed phrase

These are not all invalid; some are intentionally vocabulary questions. They should not, however, be counted as strong evidence of context-reading skill.

- **5:** “announced” versus “claimed/allegedly/supposedly”
- **26:** “just” carries most of the skeptical reading
- **41:** definition of “refute”
- **42:** definition of “skeptical”
- **56:** fixed phrase “statistically significant”
- **57:** signal words “supposedly” and “so-called”
- **62:** “ruefully” almost directly supplies the tone
- **64:** definition of “asserts”
- **67:** definition of “refutes”

## Overall judgment

- **Clearly usable as written:** 56 items
