## Module 0 — The 3 Reading Areas

**File pair:** `content/lessons/m0/abstract.csv` + `content/quizzes/m0/abstract.csv`
**Subtitle:** A 1-lesson map of the three MAP Reading areas and the tools inside each.
**Quick reference:** Reading analysis is not reading more. Learn which MAP area and reading tool the question is testing before you answer.

### Mini-Lesson

**Reading analysis is not reading more.**

Reading more helps fluency and vocabulary. But high-level reading requires something else: **noticing how the author creates meaning**.

Advanced readers ask different questions than basic readers.

| Basic reader asks | Advanced reader asks |
| --- | --- |
| What happened? | Why did the author write it this way? |
| What is the topic? | What is the central idea or theme? |
| What does this word mean? | What feeling or attitude does this word create? |
| What is the answer? | What exact evidence proves the answer? |
| What does the paragraph say? | What job does this paragraph do? |
| What is the claim? | What evidence and assumptions support it? |

The MAP Reading test scores three areas separately:

- **Literary Text** — fiction, narrative, poetry. Tests inference, tone, character, theme, figurative language.
- **Informational Text** — nonfiction, articles, history. Tests argument, structure, evidence, purpose.
- **Vocabulary** — context-based meaning, connotation, word precision.

This app teaches the tools that lift scores in those three areas.

**The core tools, by category:**

In **Literary Text**:
- **Theme** — the message about life, people, or the world.
- **Inference** — conclusions drawn from clues, not directly stated.
- **Point of View** — whose voice you're hearing.
- **Tone & Mood** — the author's attitude and the feeling the writing creates.
- **Figurative Language** — meaning beyond literal words.

In **Informational Text**:
- **Main Idea** — what the article is really saying.
- **Evidence** — the exact words that prove an answer.
- **Author's Purpose** — why the author wrote it.
- **Tone & Word Choice** — the author's attitude shown through diction.
- **Text Structure** — how the text is built.
- **Argument** — claim, evidence, reasoning.

In **Vocabulary**:
- **Context Meaning** — using surrounding sentences to decode unfamiliar words.
- **Connotation** — noticing positive, negative, or neutral feeling.
- **Figurative Word Meaning** — recognizing non-literal meaning.
- **Word Precision** — telling near-synonyms apart (suggests vs proves).
- **Academic Verbs** — naming what an author is doing.
- **Tone Vocabulary** — choosing the precise attitude word.

**Test questions hide the tool.**

A MAP question almost never says "use the Tone tool." It says something like "What attitude does the speaker show?" or "Why did the author include paragraph 3?" The first move on every advanced question is the same: **figure out which tool the question is testing.** Once you know the tool, you know what to look for.

**How to use this app.**

Pick a sub-concept. Read its short mini-lesson. Study the worked examples. Take the 10 quizzes — pay attention to the explanation for every wrong answer, not just the right one. The wrong-answer feedback is where the teaching actually happens.

Then pick another sub-concept. Then another. Over time the tools become automatic, and the questions stop feeling tricky.

### Why It Matters

Module 0 gives you the map. The rest of the app gives you each tool in turn.

## C1.1 — Context Meaning

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c1/01-context-meaning.csv` + `content/quizzes/c1/01-context-meaning.csv`
**Subtitle:** Decoding an unfamiliar word from the sentences around it.
**Quick reference:** When you hit a word you don't know, don't skip — read the sentence around it and the one after. The clues are usually right there.

**Common traps:**
- Skipping unfamiliar words instead of using the context to decode them.
- Relying only on word-parts (prefixes / roots) when the context gives clearer signal.
- Treating context decoding as guessing — when it's actually a repeatable process with named signals.

### Mini-Lesson

**Context meaning is a process, not a guess.**

Advanced readers don't have a bigger built-in dictionary. They have a better **decoding habit**. When they hit an unfamiliar word, they don't skip and they don't panic. They look at:

- **The rest of the sentence.** Words around the unknown one often define or contrast with it.
- **The sentence before and after.** The author often explains a hard word in the next breath.
- **The author's purpose.** Is this an informational text trying to teach something? Then the word is likely defined nearby. Is it a story? Then the word's meaning is shown through action or feeling.

**Four common context signals:**

- **Definition.** "The sediment, or rock and dirt washed downstream, had filled the harbor." → "rock and dirt washed downstream" defines "sediment."
- **Contrast.** "Unlike her chatty cousin, Mia was **reticent**." → "Unlike her chatty cousin" tells you reticent means the opposite of chatty.
- **Example.** "The shop sold ephemera — ticket stubs, postcards, old programs." → the list of examples shows you "ephemera" means short-lived paper items.
- **Cause / effect.** "Because the bridge had **deteriorated**, engineers closed it for repairs." → if it had to be closed for repairs, "deteriorated" means gotten worse.

**Watch out for word-parts.** Latin/Greek roots help sometimes, but they can also mislead. "Antibiotic" looks like "against life," which is roughly right. But "sanguine" doesn't mean "bloody" — it means cheerful. The text around the word is more reliable than the word's parts.

**Decoding rule:**

> Read the sentence with the unknown word. Then read the sentence before and after. Ask: what would have to be true for this whole passage to make sense? That meaning is your best guess.

### Why It Matters

Vocabulary in context is the single highest-leverage skill for breaking 250 on MAP Reading. Most plateaued readers know more words than they think — they just don't trust the decoding process.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The sediment, or rock and dirt washed downstream, had filled the harbor.
- Answer: sediment = rock and dirt washed downstream
- Explanation: The phrase after the comma is a direct definition — the author defined the hard word for you in the same sentence.

**Example 2.** Unlike her chatty cousin, Mia was reticent.
- Answer: reticent = quiet / not talkative
- Explanation: "Unlike" is a contrast signal. Whatever "reticent" means, it's the opposite of "chatty."

**Example 3.** The shop sold ephemera — ticket stubs, postcards, old programs.
- Answer: ephemera = short-lived paper items / small printed things
- Explanation: The list after the em-dash gives examples. From the examples you can infer what "ephemera" means as a category.

**Example 4.** Because the bridge had deteriorated, engineers closed it for repairs.
- Answer: deteriorated = gotten worse / fallen into bad condition
- Explanation: "Because" sets up cause and effect. If something had to be repaired, the cause word ("deteriorated") must mean "gotten worse."

## C1.2 — Connotation from Context

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c1/02-connotation-from-context.csv` + `content/quizzes/c1/02-connotation-from-context.csv`
**Subtitle:** Figuring out whether a word's feeling is positive, negative, or neutral when the word is new to you.
**Quick reference:** Even if you don't know a word, the sentence around it usually tells you whether it's a compliment, an insult, or neutral.

**Common traps:**
- Treating connotation as a memorized property of a word instead of something context reveals.
- Assuming a word is negative because the surrounding topic is negative.
- Missing how the same word can carry different connotations in different texts.

### Mini-Lesson

**Connotation is the feeling a word carries** beyond its plain definition.

Two words can mean roughly the same thing but feel very different:

- *thin* (neutral) vs *skinny* (often negative) vs *slender* (positive)
- *cheap* (negative) vs *affordable* (positive) vs *low-cost* (neutral)
- *stubborn* (negative) vs *determined* (positive) vs *fixed* (neutral)

You don't have to memorize every word's connotation. You can decode it from context.

**Three context signals for connotation:**

- **Word neighbors.** Is the word sitting next to praise-words or insult-words? "She was **stubborn** and refused to listen" → negative. "She was **stubborn** in defending what was right" → positive.
- **The author's stance.** If the author is criticizing someone, words around the unknown one are likely negative. If celebrating, likely positive.
- **The outcome described.** "His **frugal** habits saved the family thousands" — the good outcome tells you frugal is being framed positively. "His **frugal** habits embarrassed his guests" — the bad outcome tells you it's being framed negatively.

**Connotation can flip with context.** "Aggressive" is negative for a person but neutral or positive for a salesperson's strategy. "Ambitious" is positive in a job interview but can be negative in a story about someone hurting friends to get ahead. The text frames it.

**Decoding rule:**

> Look at the words near the unknown one. Are they praise or criticism? Is the outcome described good or bad? That tells you the connotation the author is using.

### Why It Matters

Connotation reveals tone and author's purpose. A student who can read connotation from context can read tone even when the specific words are unfamiliar.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** She was stubborn and refused to listen even when her own family begged her to reconsider.
- Answer: Negative
- Explanation: "Refused to listen" and "even when her own family begged" are negative neighbors. Stubborn here means inflexible in a bad way.

**Example 2.** She was stubborn in defending the truth when everyone else gave up.
- Answer: Positive
- Explanation: "Defending the truth" and "when everyone else gave up" are praise neighbors. Stubborn here means principled — same word, opposite connotation.

**Example 3.** His frugal habits saved the family thousands of dollars over the years.
- Answer: Positive
- Explanation: The good outcome ("saved the family thousands") frames frugal as wise.

**Example 4.** His frugal habits embarrassed his guests when he refused to spend on a proper meal.
- Answer: Negative
- Explanation: The bad outcome ("embarrassed his guests") frames the same word frugal as cheap or stingy.

**Example 5.** The smell from the river was acrid and made everyone cover their faces.
- Answer: Negative
- Explanation: "Made everyone cover their faces" tells you acrid is unpleasant — even if you don't know the word means harsh / bitter.

## C1.3 — Figurative Word Meaning

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c1/03-figurative-word-meaning.csv` + `content/quizzes/c1/03-figurative-word-meaning.csv`
**Subtitle:** When a familiar word is used in a non-literal sense.
**Quick reference:** When a familiar word shows up in a non-literal place — "evoke a memory," "undermine an argument," "mirror an idea" — ask what the word is doing, not just what it normally means.

**Common traps:**
- Reading figuratively-used words literally (e.g., expecting an actual mirror in a metaphorical 'mirrors').
- Treating figurative word meaning as separate from metaphor — it's a related but tighter skill: a single familiar word used non-literally.
- Missing that the figurative meaning still borrows from the literal one — the literal sense is your decoding starting point.

### Mini-Lesson

**Familiar words have figurative jobs too.**

You probably know what *undermine*, *evoke*, *mirror*, and *eclipse* mean literally. But these words appear in writing where their literal meaning makes no sense:

- ""The evidence **undermined** the prosecutor's case.""
- ""The poem **evokes** the smell of rain.""
- ""The novel **mirrors** the chaos of the 1960s.""
- ""Her promotion **eclipsed** her brother's achievement.""

No one is digging under a wall, the poem does not literally produce rain, no novel is a mirror, no eclipse is happening. The author is using these words **figuratively** — borrowing the literal idea and applying it abstractly.

**The mental move:**

When you see a familiar word in a context that doesn't fit its literal meaning, don't read it literally. Ask: *what is this word doing here?* Then borrow the literal meaning's shape:

- *undermine* literally = dig under a wall to weaken it → figuratively = weaken (an argument, a claim, a position)
- *evoke* literally = call forth → figuratively = bring to mind (a feeling, a memory)
- *mirror* literally = reflect like a mirror → figuratively = represent, show, parallel
- *eclipse* literally = block the sun → figuratively = outshine, surpass
- *anchor* literally = hold a ship in place → figuratively = give stability or grounding

**Common figurative verbs to watch for:**

*undermine, evoke, mirror, eclipse, anchor, weigh, fuel, harvest, ignite, paint, color, frame, bridge, channel, target, navigate, weather*

Each has a literal job AND a figurative one. The context tells you which.

### Why It Matters

Figurative word meaning is constant in MAP informational and literary texts. Reading these words literally — or skipping them — costs comprehension at exactly the level the test rewards.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The evidence undermined the prosecutor's case.
- Answer: Figurative — undermined = weakened
- Explanation: No one is digging here. "Undermined" borrows from its literal sense (digging under a wall) and applies it to the case, meaning the evidence weakened the case.

**Example 2.** The poem evokes the smell of rain.
- Answer: Figurative — evokes = brings to mind
- Explanation: A poem can't literally call a smell into existence. "Evokes" means the language brings the memory or sense of rain to the reader's mind.

**Example 3.** Her promotion eclipsed her brother's achievement.
- Answer: Figurative — eclipsed = surpassed / outshone
- Explanation: No actual eclipse. The promotion outshone the brother's achievement, the way an eclipse blocks the sun.

**Example 4.** Stable funding anchors the entire program.
- Answer: Figurative — anchors = stabilizes / grounds
- Explanation: No ship, no anchor. Stable funding holds the program steady the way a literal anchor holds a ship.

**Example 5.** Her arguments mirror those of her mentor.
- Answer: Figurative — mirror = match closely
- Explanation: No reflection. Her arguments closely match her mentor's, the way a mirror reflects exactly.

## C2.1 — Word Precision

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c2/01-word-precision.csv` + `content/quizzes/c2/01-word-precision.csv`
**Subtitle:** Telling near-synonyms apart.
**Quick reference:** Near-synonyms aren't interchangeable. "Suggests" is weaker than "proves." "Reluctant" is hesitating; "unwilling" is refusing. The author's choice signals something.

**Common traps:**
- Treating near-synonyms as fully interchangeable.
- Missing the certainty signal in a hedge verb ("suggests" or "claims") versus a demonstration verb ("proves" or "shows").
- Mixing up size words ("substantial") with importance words ("significant").

### Mini-Lesson

**Words that look the same aren't always the same.**

Advanced readers know that English has many words that look like synonyms but carry **different shades of meaning**. When an author chooses one over another, they're signaling something.

**Common pairs that look interchangeable but aren't:**

- *suggests* vs *proves* — suggests hints; proves demonstrates with certainty.
- *implies* vs *states* — implies hints indirectly; states says directly.
- *claims* vs *proves* — claims = asserts (not yet shown); proves = demonstrates.
- *concedes* vs *admits* — concedes is usually in argument; admits is more general.
- *reluctant* vs *unwilling* — reluctant = hesitating; unwilling = refusing.
- *ambiguous* vs *unclear* — ambiguous = has multiple possible meanings; unclear = hard to understand.
- *requires* vs *needs* — requires = mandatory; needs = useful or necessary.
- *contributes* vs *causes* — contributes = adds to; causes = produces directly.
- *substantial* vs *significant* — substantial = large in size; significant = important in effect.

**Why this matters on tests:**

A test question might ask whether the author *proves* or just *suggests* something. If you read those words as the same, you'll miss the question. Authors are careful with these distinctions — readers should be too.

**The discrimination move:**

Read the sentence and ask: *how certain is the author?* *How strong is the claim?* *Is the action mandatory or just preferred?* The author chose the precise word; you have to read at the same precision.

### Why It Matters

Plateaued readers often see near-synonyms as interchangeable. They're not — and MAP questions exploit that.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The data suggests the policy works.
- Answer: Suggests = hints; the data doesn't prove it.
- Explanation: If the author had wanted to claim proof, they would have said "proves" or "shows." "Suggests" hedges.

**Example 2.** She was reluctant to leave, but she eventually agreed.
- Answer: Reluctant = hesitant, not refusing.
- Explanation: If she were unwilling, she wouldn't have agreed. Reluctant means she resisted at first but came around.

**Example 3.** The instructions were ambiguous — they could be read two different ways.
- Answer: Ambiguous = has multiple possible meanings.
- Explanation: If the instructions were merely unclear (hard to understand), the writer wouldn't specify "two different ways."

**Example 4.** He conceded that his original argument had been flawed.
- Answer: Conceded = admitted (in an argument context).
- Explanation: "Conceded" is the word used in debate and argument when someone gives ground. "Admitted" would work but loses the argument context.

**Example 5.** The traffic plan contributes to congestion but is not the only cause.
- Answer: Contributes = adds to (one of several causes).
- Explanation: If the plan CAUSED the congestion alone, the sentence would say "causes." "Contributes" signals it's one factor among many.

## C3.1 — Academic Verbs

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c3/01-academic-verbs.csv` + `content/quizzes/c3/01-academic-verbs.csv`
**Subtitle:** The verbs that tell you what the author is doing.
**Quick reference:** Watch the author's verbs. They tell you the move: assert, imply, concede, scrutinize, undermine, refute, emphasize, contrast. The verb is half the meaning.

**Common traps:**
- Glossing over the author's verbs because they're not difficult words individually.
- Treating "asserts" and "proves" as the same — they're not (asserts = states confidently; proves = demonstrates with evidence).
- Missing when the academic verb is the giveaway for the question's answer.

### Mini-Lesson

**The author's verbs are signals.**

Advanced texts and test questions are full of academic verbs. They name what the author or a character is *doing*. If you skim past them, you miss half the meaning.

**High-frequency academic verbs and what they signal:**

- **assert** — state confidently; the assertion may or may not be proven by surrounding evidence.
- **imply** — hint at something without saying it directly.
- **concede** — admit something (often in an argument).
- **scrutinize** — examine carefully or critically.
- **undermine** — weaken (a claim, position, argument).
- **refute** — disprove or argue against.
- **emphasize** — give particular weight or attention.
- **contrast** — show differences between two things.
- **illustrate** — give an example to show.
- **acknowledge** — recognize that something is true (often a counterpoint).
- **clarify** — make clearer; remove ambiguity.
- **dismiss** — reject as unimportant.

**Why these matter:**

Test questions often hinge on the verb. ""The author *asserts*..."" is different from ""The author *proves*..."" One claims; the other demonstrates. ""The author *concedes*..."" is different from ""The author *insists*..."" One gives ground; the other holds firm.

**The reading move:**

When you see one of these verbs, pause and ask:

- What's the author doing?
- How strong or weak is this verb?
- Does this match what the surrounding text actually does?

If a question asks ""What does the author do in paragraph 3?"" the answer is almost certainly one of these verbs.

### Why It Matters

Academic verbs are the vocabulary of MAP-style questions. Without this lexicon, students can spot a paragraph's job but can't name it.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The author asserts that screen time harms attention spans.
- Answer: Asserts = states confidently
- Explanation: "Asserts" is a claim verb. The author is putting forward a position; surrounding evidence determines whether it is proven.

**Example 2.** The data implies a connection between sleep and grades.
- Answer: Implies = hints indirectly
- Explanation: "Implies" is the hedged version of "shows." The data points toward a connection without explicitly stating one.

**Example 3.** She conceded that her opponent had raised a valid point.
- Answer: Conceded = admitted in an argument
- Explanation: "Conceded" is the argument-context version of "admitted." She's giving ground in a debate.

**Example 4.** The report scrutinizes the city's spending over five years.
- Answer: Scrutinizes = examines carefully and critically
- Explanation: "Scrutinizes" means more than "looks at" — it implies careful, critical attention.

**Example 5.** This evidence undermines the company's earlier claim.
- Answer: Undermines = weakens
- Explanation: "Undermines" is the figurative weaken-from-below verb. The evidence isn't refuting outright; it's chipping at the foundation.

## C3.2 — Tone Vocabulary

**File pair:** `content/lessons/c3/02-tone-vocabulary.csv` + `content/quizzes/c3/02-tone-vocabulary.csv`
**Subtitle:** The lexicon that lets you name the attitude you see.
**Quick reference:** You can sense an attitude without being able to name it. Tone vocabulary is the lexicon — skeptical, nostalgic, dismissive, urgent, reflective — that lets you label what you see correctly.

**Common traps:**
- Spotting the attitude correctly but picking a tone word that's too strong or too weak.
- Defaulting to "neutral" or "negative" because no more specific word comes to mind.
- Mixing up emotionally similar tone words (skeptical vs scornful; reflective vs nostalgic).

### Mini-Lesson

**Spotting tone is one skill. Naming it is another.**

Plateaued readers often sense the attitude in a passage but pick the wrong tone word because they don't have a wide enough lexicon. The fix is direct: **learn the words advanced readers use to name tone.**

**A working tone-word lexicon:**

*Approving / positive attitudes:*
- **admiring** — full of praise
- **approving** — endorsing, supporting
- **sympathetic** — feeling for, on the side of
- **hopeful** — expecting good
- **respectful** — showing high regard

*Disapproving / negative attitudes:*
- **critical** — pointing out flaws
- **skeptical** — doubting (often quietly)
- **dismissive** — brushing aside as unimportant
- **scornful** — openly contemptuous
- **alarmed** — concerned about danger

*Emotionally weighted:*
- **urgent** — time-pressured, demanding action
- **passionate** — intense, deeply felt
- **bitter** — angry over past wrongs
- **despairing** — without hope

*Reflective stances:*
- **nostalgic** — fondly looking back
- **reflective** — thoughtful, considering
- **wry** — drily humorous, knowing
- **ironic** — saying one thing, meaning another
- **cautious** — careful, hedged

**The naming move:**

When you've spotted the attitude:

1. Is it positive, negative, or neither?
2. How strong is it — quiet doubt, or open contempt?
3. Is it looking forward (urgent, hopeful) or backward (nostalgic, regretful)?

That triangulation usually lands you on the right word.

**Watch out for near-synonyms.** *Skeptical* (quiet doubt) ≠ *scornful* (open contempt). *Reflective* (thoughtful) ≠ *nostalgic* (longing). Get specific.

### Why It Matters

A wide tone-word lexicon converts vague intuition ("the author seems annoyed") into precise answers ("the author is dismissive"). MAP questions reward precision.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The reviewer praised every detail of the performance, calling it the most moving production of the season.
- Answer: Admiring
- Explanation: Strong praise, no qualifications — that's admiring, not just approving.

**Example 2.** The official brushed off the complaints with a quick "that's just how it is," then changed the subject.
- Answer: Dismissive
- Explanation: Brushing off and changing the subject is the action of dismissing — treating concerns as unimportant.

**Example 3.** The author wonders, fifty years later, whether the small town she grew up in still has the same bakery on the corner.
- Answer: Nostalgic
- Explanation: Looking back fondly at a specific small detail from the past = nostalgic.

**Example 4.** The report warns that without action this month, the damage may be irreversible.
- Answer: Urgent
- Explanation: Time pressure ("this month") + warning of irreversible damage = urgent.

**Example 5.** The professor doubted, though politely, whether the new method was as effective as advertised.
- Answer: Skeptical
- Explanation: "Doubted" + "politely" = quiet doubt, not open contempt. That's skeptical, not scornful.

## A1.1 — Theme Is a Message, Not a Topic

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a1/01-theme-message-not-topic.csv` + `content/quizzes/a1/01-theme-message-not-topic.csv`
**Subtitle:** Theme is a complete claim about life — not just a one-word subject.
**Quick reference:** If you can say the theme in one word, it's still a topic. Theme is a full sentence the story is making about life, people, or the world.

**Common traps:**
- Naming a topic word (friendship, courage, loss) and calling it a theme.
- Writing a theme as plot summary instead of an abstract message.
- Picking a theme that's only supported by one event in the story.

### Mini-Lesson

**Topic = what the story is ABOUT. Theme = what the story is SAYING about it.**

Plateaued readers often write "the theme is friendship" or "the theme is courage." Those are **topics**. A theme is a complete claim the story is making:

- *Topic:* friendship → *Theme:* true friendship requires honesty, even when honesty hurts.
- *Topic:* courage → *Theme:* courage often shows up in small, private moments rather than public ones.
- *Topic:* family → *Theme:* the people who raise us shape what we think "normal" looks like, for better or worse.

A theme has a subject and a claim. ""Friendship"" has no claim. ""True friendship requires honesty"" does.

**The single-sentence test:**

If you can finish this sentence, you have a theme:

> The story is making the claim that ___.

If you can only fill in a single word, you have a topic.

**Theme should be defensible from the text.**

A theme isn't a guess. It's a claim you can support with what actually happens. After you write the theme as a sentence, check: do the story's events, characters, and ending point toward this message? If not, you've got the wrong theme.

**A theme is usually universal.**

The theme of *Charlotte's Web* isn't "a pig and a spider become friends." That's the plot. The theme is something closer to "true friendship asks for sacrifice." Notice that the theme could apply to other stories with no pigs and no spiders.

### Why It Matters

Theme is one of the most common literary questions on MAP — and "topic instead of theme" is its #1 wrong-answer trap.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Friendship
- Answer: Topic, not theme.
- Explanation: A one-word answer is a topic. To become a theme, it needs a claim — what is the story SAYING about friendship?

**Example 2.** True friendship requires honesty, even when honesty is hard.
- Answer: Theme.
- Explanation: Complete sentence + claim about life + applies beyond this specific story. That's the theme shape.

**Example 3.** Courage often appears in small, private moments rather than dramatic public ones.
- Answer: Theme.
- Explanation: Names the topic (courage) AND makes a claim about it (where it appears). Universal — applies beyond the specific story.

**Example 4.** The boy saves the dog from the river.
- Answer: Plot, not theme.
- Explanation: This is what happened. A theme is the message the story makes about what happened (e.g., "taking risks for others reveals who we really are").

**Example 5.** Loss is part of being alive.
- Answer: Theme.
- Explanation: Topic (loss) + claim about life + universal. Theme shape.

## A1.2 — Theme Is Supported by Multiple Details

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a1/02-theme-multiple-details.csv` + `content/quizzes/a1/02-theme-multiple-details.csv`
**Subtitle:** A theme is the message the whole story makes — not the lesson from one moment.
**Quick reference:** A theme should be supported by SEVERAL events, characters, or details in the story — not just one. If only one moment supports it, you probably have a plot point, not a theme.

**Common traps:**
- Drawing a theme from one moment instead of the whole story.
- Mistaking the most dramatic scene's lesson for the story's overall theme.
- Picking a theme that sounds deep but isn't supported by what actually happens.

### Mini-Lesson

**A theme is the story's overall claim — not the lesson from one scene.**

When students pick a theme, they often grab the most dramatic moment and treat it as the message. That usually misses the theme. The real test:

> *Can I point to at least three places in the story that support this theme?*

If you can only point to one event, you've probably picked a **plot point**, not a theme.

**How to find a defensible theme:**

1. Skim for **repeated patterns**: a feeling that returns, a choice that's made more than once, an object or image that recurs.
2. Watch the **ending**. What changed? What did the characters learn? Themes usually become clearest at the end.
3. Look for **multiple moments** that point in the same direction. Multiple characters can help, but a single protagonist's arc across several scenes can also support a theme.

**Testing your theme draft:**

After writing a theme as a sentence, list 3 specific moments from the story that support it. If you can do that, the theme is defensible. If you can only list one moment, the theme is too narrow.

**Themes can have several supporting lines.**

Most rich stories have more than one theme. A story about a family across generations might support "the people who raised us shape what we accept as normal" AND "some patterns can be broken if someone is brave enough to name them." Both are themes — both supported by multiple details.

### Why It Matters

MAP questions often ask "Which theme is BEST supported by the passage?" — the answer is the one with multiple supporting details, not the most dramatic-sounding option.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** A character apologizes once at the end of a story.
- Answer: One moment — probably plot, not a defensible theme yet.
- Explanation: One apology doesn't establish a theme. Check the rest of the story for related details before deciding.

**Example 2.** Three different characters each face the same kind of choice and respond differently.
- Answer: Strong basis for a theme — the pattern is the theme signal.
- Explanation: Pattern across multiple characters is a classic theme signal. The author is making a claim about how people respond to that kind of choice.

**Example 3.** A specific object (a locket, a photograph, a song) appears in three different scenes.
- Answer: Strong theme signal — recurring objects usually carry meaning.
- Explanation: Recurrence creates emphasis. Whatever the object stands for is likely part of the theme.

**Example 4.** The main character changes how they think about something between the beginning and the end.
- Answer: Strong theme signal — the change itself often IS the theme.
- Explanation: Character change across the arc is one of the most reliable theme indicators. What changed = what the story is claiming about.

## A2.1 — Inference = Clue + Reasoning

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a2/01-inference-clue-reasoning.csv` + `content/quizzes/a2/01-inference-clue-reasoning.csv`
**Subtitle:** Inference is conclusion + the specific clue that supports it.
**Quick reference:** An inference isn't a guess. It's a clue from the text plus the reasoning that connects the clue to the conclusion. If you can't name the clue, you don't have an inference.

**Common traps:**
- Making a conclusion without naming the specific clue from the text.
- Using outside knowledge instead of staying inside the text.
- Confusing a guess (no clue) with an inference (clue + reasoning).

### Mini-Lesson

**Inference has two parts: a clue and reasoning.**

A weak student reads "Mia checked the clock for the third time and tapped her pencil" and answers: *"Mia is nervous."*

An advanced reader answers: *"Mia is nervous, because checking the clock three times and tapping her pencil show physical impatience."*

Both reach the same conclusion. Only one shows the work. On a test, only one earns full credit.

**The formula:**

> Inference = (clue from the text) + (reasoning that connects the clue to the conclusion).

If you can't name the clue, what you have is a **guess**, not an inference.

**Types of clues to look for:**

- **Actions** — what a character does (especially what they do repeatedly or what they avoid).
- **Dialogue** — what a character says, how they say it, what they don't say.
- **Setting details** — weather, time of day, condition of objects.
- **Reactions** — how other characters respond.
- **Word choice** — the narrator's specific verbs and adjectives.

**The check:**

After making an inference, ask:

> What in the text would I point to if someone asked me to prove it?

If the answer is "I just feel it," you've made a guess. If you can name a specific clue, you've made an inference.

**Inferences should not require outside knowledge.**

The text provides everything you need. If your inference depends on "well, I know from real life that..." — pull back. Stay inside the text.

### Why It Matters

Inference questions are constant in MAP literary reading. The trap students fall into is making a correct-feeling conclusion without grounding it in a specific text clue.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Mia checked the clock for the third time and tapped her pencil against the desk.
- Answer: She is nervous. Clue: checking the clock three times AND tapping pencil. Reasoning: both behaviors are physical signs of impatience or anxiety.
- Explanation: Strong inference — names two specific clues and explains why they point to nervousness.

**Example 2.** "I'm fine," she said, but she didn't look up from her phone.
- Answer: She is NOT fine. Clue: she says "I'm fine" but avoids eye contact. Reasoning: the gap between what she says and what her body does suggests the words aren't true.
- Explanation: Strong inference — the clue is the gap between dialogue and action, and the reasoning explains why that gap matters.

**Example 3.** The kitchen was clean — too clean. The counters had been wiped twice and the dish rack was empty.
- Answer: Someone has cleaned the kitchen recently, possibly under stress or in preparation for something. Clue: "too clean" + wiped twice + empty rack. Reasoning: that level of cleanliness is unusual; the narrator notes it as notable.
- Explanation: The narrator's word choice ("too clean") signals it's worth noting. The reasoning explains why over-cleaning matters.

**Example 4.** "He'll come," Dad said, glancing at the door for the fourth time that hour.
- Answer: Dad isn't actually sure. Clue: glancing at the door FOUR times in an hour. Reasoning: repeated checking suggests doubt, even though his words say confidence.
- Explanation: Action contradicts dialogue. The clue is the checking; the reasoning is the gap between words and behavior.

## A2.2 — Inference from Character Action

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a2/02-inference-from-character-action.csv` + `content/quizzes/a2/02-inference-from-character-action.csv`
**Subtitle:** What characters DO reveals what they FEEL — even when they don't say it.
**Quick reference:** Watch what characters do, especially what they do repeatedly or what they avoid. Actions reveal emotion that dialogue often hides.

**Common traps:**
- Restating the action instead of inferring the feeling it implies.
- Missing repeated actions (which signal intensity).
- Missing mismatched actions (which signal hidden feeling).

### Mini-Lesson

**Actions speak louder than dialogue.**

In fiction, characters rarely state their feelings directly. Instead, the author shows feelings through what characters DO:

- A character who keeps glancing at the door → expecting someone, anxious about someone.
- A character who pushes food around the plate without eating → upset, distracted.
- A character who laughs too loudly at a joke → performing, uncomfortable.
- A character who answers a question with another question → avoiding.

**The advanced-reader move:**

When you read an action, ask:

> What feeling would cause someone to do THIS?

Don't restate the action. Translate it into the feeling.

**Watch especially for:**

- **Repeated actions** — checking the clock three times, pacing, rereading a letter. Repetition signals intensity.
- **Avoided actions** — not looking up, not answering, not opening the door. Avoidance signals a feeling too strong to face.
- **Mismatched actions** — laughing at sad news, smiling when angry. Mismatches signal that what's on the surface isn't what's underneath.
- **Small physical actions** — clenching fists, holding breath, looking away. The body shows what the words won't.

**Common trap: just restating the action.**

Question: *"How does Sam feel after his sister leaves?"*
Weak answer: *"He stares out the window."*
Strong answer: *"He's lonely and missing her — he stares out the window long after she's gone, as if hoping she'll come back."*

The weak answer describes what happened. The strong answer infers from it.

### Why It Matters

Most literary inference questions on MAP hinge on this skill — reading actions as evidence of feeling.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** After his sister moved out, Sam stayed in the doorway of her empty room for ten minutes before turning out the light.
- Answer: Sam is sad and missing her. Clue: staying in the doorway of an EMPTY room for ten minutes. Reasoning: lingering in a space connected to someone is a classic action of grief or longing.
- Explanation: Strong inference — the action (lingering) is named, and the reasoning explains why it points to sadness rather than something else.

**Example 2.** Maya laughed when her friend told her about the broken phone. Then she stopped laughing and offered to help pay for the repair.
- Answer: Maya regretted the laugh and felt sympathy. Clue: the SHIFT from laughing to offering help. Reasoning: the change in behavior shows she realized the laugh was wrong and chose to make it right.
- Explanation: The mismatched first action + the corrective second action together reveal the feeling — guilt followed by sympathy.

**Example 3.** Dad opened the bill, closed it, opened it again, then set it on the kitchen counter without saying anything.
- Answer: Dad is worried about the bill. Clue: opening it twice + setting it down without comment. Reasoning: rereading suggests the amount is hard to absorb; silence suggests he doesn't want to share the worry yet.
- Explanation: Two actions together (rereading + silence) make a stronger inference than either alone.

**Example 4.** Tara didn't open the letter all day. She set it on the table at breakfast, walked past it at lunch, and finally took it to her room before dinner.
- Answer: Tara is afraid of what the letter says. Clue: avoiding it ALL DAY before finally taking it. Reasoning: avoidance signals a feeling too strong to face right away.
- Explanation: Repeated avoidance is the clue. Eventually carrying it away suggests she'll face it privately.

## A2.3 — Inference vs Stated Fact

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a2/03-inference-vs-stated-fact.csv` + `content/quizzes/a2/03-inference-vs-stated-fact.csv`
**Subtitle:** If the text says it, it's not an inference.
**Quick reference:** An inference is a conclusion the text SUGGESTS without saying. If the answer is just what the story explicitly states, it's a fact — not an inference. Read the question carefully.

**Common traps:**
- Picking a directly stated fact when the question asks for an inference.
- Missing the question's wording ("infer," "suggest," "conclude") that signals an inference is wanted.
- Thinking that an answer that's TRUE in the text is automatically the correct inference.

### Mini-Lesson

**Inference is what the text SUGGESTS — not what it STATES.**

A common trap: a question asks for an inference, and the student picks a true statement from the text — but the text already SAID it. That's a stated fact, not an inference.

Inference means **going one step beyond** what the text says — using a clue from the text to reach a conclusion the text doesn't say outright.

**The test:**

> Is this answer DIRECTLY in the text, or do I have to use a clue plus reasoning to reach it?

- Directly stated = a fact.
- Reached through clue + reasoning = an inference.

**Example:**

Text: *"Mia checked the clock for the third time and tapped her pencil. The room was quiet except for the hum of the radiator."*

Question: *"Which of the following can be inferred?"*

- *Wrong answer choice:* "The radiator hums." — STATED. The text says this directly.
- *Right answer choice:* "Mia is anxious about something." — INFERRED. The text doesn't say "Mia is anxious." The reader has to use the clock-checking and pencil-tapping as clues.

**Watch for these question wordings:**

- "Which of the following can be **inferred** from the passage?" — looking for something not stated.
- "The passage **suggests** that..." — looking for an inference.
- "It can be **concluded** that..." — usually inference.
- "The passage **states** that..." — looking for a directly stated fact.

If the question asks for an inference, throw out any answer that's directly quoted or paraphrased from the text. Look for the one that requires a clue plus reasoning.

### Why It Matters

MAP frequently asks "which can be inferred?" and includes wrong answers that are TRUE but STATED. Students who don't make the inference/fact distinction lose these points.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Text: "Mia checked the clock for the third time and tapped her pencil. The room was quiet except for the hum of the radiator." Question: Which of the following can be INFERRED?
- Answer: Mia is anxious about something. (NOT "the radiator hums.")
- Explanation: "The radiator hums" is stated. An inference requires reasoning from a clue — Mia's behavior gives the clue; "anxious" is the conclusion.

**Example 2.** Text: "The boy hadn't eaten since breakfast. He stared at the menu for a long time before finally ordering a glass of water." Question: Which can be INFERRED?
- Answer: The boy may not have money for food.
- Explanation: The text states he hasn't eaten and that he ordered only water. The INFERENCE — that he may lack money — uses those clues to reach a conclusion the text doesn't state outright.

**Example 3.** Text: "It had rained for three days. The river was higher than anyone could remember." Question: Which can be INFERRED?
- Answer: Flooding may happen if the rain continues.
- Explanation: The text states the rain and the high river. The INFERENCE uses those facts to reach a conclusion the text doesn't state — that flooding is a risk.

**Example 4.** Text: "Anna walked her brother to school every morning that fall." Question: Which is a STATED FACT, not an inference?
- Answer: Anna walked her brother to school in the fall.
- Explanation: Directly stated — no clue plus reasoning needed. An inference WOULD be: "Anna's brother was likely younger or new to the school" — that's reached by reasoning.

### Quiz Items

## A2.4 — Avoiding Over-Inference

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a2/04-avoiding-over-inference.csv` + `content/quizzes/a2/04-avoiding-over-inference.csv`
**Subtitle:** Only infer as far as the text supports — don't pull in outside knowledge or extrapolate too far.
**Quick reference:** Inference works inside the text. The moment you reach for outside knowledge or a conclusion the clues don't support, you've over-inferred. Stay close to the page.

**Common traps:**
- Adding outside knowledge to an inference ("in real life, this would mean...").
- Extrapolating beyond what the clue supports (small clue, big conclusion).
- Picking a dramatic-sounding answer that goes further than the text justifies.

### Mini-Lesson

**Over-inference is the most common advanced-reader mistake.**

Plateaued readers know inference is important — but they often go TOO FAR. They reach conclusions the text doesn't quite support, usually by leaning on outside knowledge.

**Two forms of over-inference:**

1. **Outside knowledge.** You add information from your own life or general knowledge that the text doesn't give you.
   - Text: *"Mia is a fifth grader who loves to read."*
   - Over-inference: *"Mia probably gets straight A's."*
   - The text doesn't say anything about her grades. "Loves to read" doesn't necessarily mean A's.

2. **Extrapolation beyond the clue.** You take a small clue and stretch it into a much bigger conclusion than the clue supports.
   - Text: *"He frowned at the menu and ordered water."*
   - Over-inference: *"His family is bankrupt and he's about to drop out of school."*
   - The clue supports something — maybe he's not hungry, or not buying. It doesn't support that big a story.

**The test:**

After making an inference, ask:

> Is this the SMALLEST conclusion the clue supports, or am I adding stuff?

The smallest, closest-to-the-text conclusion is usually the right inference. The bigger, more dramatic one is usually over-inference.

**Trap-spotting:**

Wrong-answer choices in test questions often include over-inferences. They sound smart but they go further than the text supports. If you find yourself thinking "well, in real life this would mean...", pull back — that's outside knowledge.

**Rule:**

> Stay one step beyond the text. Not two. Not three.

### Why It Matters

Over-inference is the trap that catches advanced readers more than basic ones — basic readers under-infer, but the next level is to infer the RIGHT amount.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Text: "Mia is a fifth grader who loves to read." Over-inference: "Mia gets straight A's."
- Answer: Over-inference — outside knowledge.
- Explanation: Loving to read doesn't tell us about grades. The reader added information that isn't in the text.

**Example 2.** Text: "He frowned at the menu and ordered water." Over-inference: "His family is bankrupt and he's about to drop out of school."
- Answer: Over-inference — extrapolation.
- Explanation: The clue (frown + water order) supports something small (maybe not hungry, maybe saving money). It doesn't support a whole life crisis.

**Example 3.** Text: "The grandmother smiled when she saw the photograph." Reasonable inference: "The photograph means something to her."
- Answer: Reasonable — small, close to the text.
- Explanation: The smile is the clue. The smallest inference is that the photo matters. We don't know WHY yet; further conclusions would be over-inference.

**Example 4.** Text: "Tom didn't come to school for three days." Over-inference: "Tom is in serious trouble at home."
- Answer: Over-inference.
- Explanation: The text gives one fact (three absences). It doesn't support a specific cause. "Tom may have been sick or had something going on" would be a more defensible inference.

## A4.1 — Tone vs Topic in Fiction

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a4/01-tone-vs-topic-fiction.csv` + `content/quizzes/a4/01-tone-vs-topic-fiction.csv`
**Subtitle:** Topic = what the story is about. Tone = how the author or narrator feels about it.
**Quick reference:** Don't confuse what a story is ABOUT with how the author FEELS about it. Two stories about the same topic can have completely different tones.

**Common traps:**
- Naming the topic when the question asks for tone.
- Picking a generic word like "negative" instead of a precise tone word.
- Treating the character's mood as the same thing as the narrator's tone.

### Mini-Lesson

**Topic = subject. Tone = attitude.**

Two short stories both about a grandmother's death. One has a tender, nostalgic tone. The other has a bitter, regretful tone. **Same topic, different tones.**

The topic is just what the story is about — death, friendship, school, growing up. The tone is the author's or narrator's **attitude** toward the topic, shown through word choice and what the author chooses to emphasize.

**Spotting tone in fiction:**

- **Word choice.** Does the narrator describe the grandmother's hands as "worn" (sympathetic) or "old" (neutral) or "useless" (negative)? The adjective is the tone signal.
- **What's emphasized.** A nostalgic story lingers on warm details (her laugh, the smell of her kitchen). A bitter story lingers on what was missed or denied.
- **The narrator's verbs.** "She gave" vs "she withheld." "He smiled" vs "he smirked." Verbs carry tone.

**The naming move:**

After spotting tone, name it precisely. "Negative" is too broad — be specific:

- *bitter* — angry over past wrongs
- *nostalgic* — fondly remembering
- *wistful* — gently sad about something lost
- *celebratory* — full of praise or joy
- *tender* — gentle, caring
- *sober* — serious, somber

**Common trap:**

A student asked "What is the tone of this passage?" answers "death" or "grandmother." Those are topics. The tone is HOW THE AUTHOR FEELS about death or the grandmother — and the right answer is a tone word, not a subject word.

### Why It Matters

Plateaued readers often confuse what a story is about with how the author feels about it. Tone-vs-topic is the foundational tone skill in literary reading.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The narrator describes her grandmother's hands as "worn from years of giving, knotted but still warm." Topic: ___. Tone: ___.
- Answer: Topic: grandmother's hands. Tone: tender / loving.
- Explanation: "Worn from years of giving" and "still warm" are tender word choices. The author's attitude toward the grandmother is loving.

**Example 2.** The narrator describes her grandmother's hands as "old, twisted, the hands of someone who never reached for me." Topic: ___. Tone: ___.
- Answer: Topic: grandmother's hands (same as above). Tone: bitter / hurt.
- Explanation: Same topic as the previous example, completely different tone. "Never reached for me" reveals the narrator's wound; the tone is bitter, not tender.

**Example 3.** The narrator: "That summer we lived three blocks from the bakery, and the smell of bread woke us every morning." Topic: ___. Tone: ___.
- Answer: Topic: a summer at a specific house. Tone: nostalgic.
- Explanation: Sensory detail (smell of bread, every morning) and the past-summer framing are classic nostalgic moves.

**Example 4.** A character is angry, but the narrator describes the scene calmly: "He spoke without raising his voice; he did not need to." Topic: ___. Tone: ___.
- Answer: Topic: a confrontation. Narrator's tone: sober / restrained (NOT angry).
- Explanation: Trap: the character's mood (angry) is different from the narrator's tone (sober). The narrator's word choice (calm, restrained) sets the tone, not the character's emotion.

## A4.3 — Mood vs Tone

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a4/03-mood-vs-tone.csv` + `content/quizzes/a4/03-mood-vs-tone.csv`
**Subtitle:** Tone is author-side attitude. Mood is reader-side feeling.
**Quick reference:** Tone is the author or narrator's attitude TOWARD the subject. Mood is the FEELING the writing creates in the reader. They can be related but they're not the same.

**Common traps:**
- Treating tone and mood as the same thing.
- Picking a mood word when the question asks for tone (or vice versa).
- Forgetting that the narrator's tone can differ from the mood the scene creates.

### Mini-Lesson

**Tone and mood are NOT the same thing.**

This is one of the most-confused distinctions in literary reading. Here's the rule:

- **Tone** is the **author's or narrator's attitude** toward the subject. (Author-side.)
- **Mood** is the **feeling the writing creates in the reader**. (Reader-side.)

**Example:**

A horror story might have a **calm, matter-of-fact tone** (the narrator describes events without panic) but a **terrifying mood** (the reader feels scared while reading). Tone and mood are doing different jobs.

**Another example:**

A nostalgic memoir might have a **wistful tone** (the writer's gentle attitude toward the past) AND a **wistful mood** (the reader is left feeling a little sad too). Here tone and mood are similar — but they're still two different things.

**How to tell them apart on a test:**

- If the question asks about the **author's attitude**: → tone.
- If the question asks about how the **passage makes the reader feel**: → mood.
- If the question asks about the **atmosphere of the setting**: → mood (atmosphere is a kind of mood).

**Watch the question wording:**

- "What is the author's tone?" → tone
- "What mood does the passage create?" → mood
- "The narrator's attitude toward..." → tone
- "The reader is left feeling..." → mood
- "What is the atmosphere of the scene?" → mood

**Tone words and mood words often overlap** (tense, cheerful, sad) — but the QUESTION tells you which is being asked. Read carefully.

**Most common confusion:**

Students see a scary scene and answer "the tone is scary." But the narrator might be describing the scary scene calmly. The MOOD is scary (what the reader feels). The TONE might be calm (the narrator's attitude). Two different answers, both correct for their respective questions.

### Why It Matters

Mood-vs-tone is a frequent advanced-reader trap. Tests reward students who read the question carefully and don't conflate the two.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** A horror story narrator describes a haunting in calm, factual prose: "The door opened by itself for the third time that night."
- Answer: Tone: calm / matter-of-fact. Mood: tense / eerie.
- Explanation: Two different answers — and both are correct for their respective questions. The narrator's attitude is calm; the feeling created in the reader is tense.

**Example 2.** A nostalgic memoir: "That summer we lived three blocks from the bakery, and the smell of bread woke us every morning."
- Answer: Tone: nostalgic. Mood: nostalgic / wistful.
- Explanation: Here tone and mood are similar — but still two different things. The author's attitude toward the past is nostalgic; the writing leaves the reader feeling wistful.

**Example 3.** A scene set in an empty house at dusk: "The boards under her feet creaked once. The light from the open back door grew thinner."
- Answer: Tone: spare / detached. Mood: uneasy / suspenseful.
- Explanation: The narrator's tone is restrained ("creaked once," "grew thinner") but the scene creates an uneasy mood in the reader. Different things.

**Example 4.** Test question A: "What is the author's tone?" Test question B: "What mood does the passage create?"
- Answer: Two different question types. A wants a tone word. B wants a mood/atmosphere word.
- Explanation: Read the wording carefully. The same passage might have a tone answer ("sober") and a mood answer ("melancholy") that don't perfectly match.

## A5.1 — Imagery Creates Meaning

**File pair:** `content/lessons/a5/01-imagery-creates-meaning.csv` + `content/quizzes/a5/01-imagery-creates-meaning.csv`
**Subtitle:** Sensory detail isn't decoration. It shapes feeling, tone, and theme.
**Quick reference:** When you see vivid sensory detail — a smell, a sound, a texture, a color — ask what feeling or meaning the author is building. Imagery is doing a job, not just describing.

**Common traps:**
- Treating imagery as decoration rather than as meaning-making work.
- Naming what the imagery shows literally instead of what feeling/meaning it creates.
- Missing recurring imagery as a theme signal.

### Mini-Lesson

**Imagery is not decoration.**

When an author uses sensory detail — what something smells like, sounds like, feels like, looks like, tastes like — they're not just describing for description's sake. They're creating a feeling, building a tone, or pointing toward a theme.

**The reading move:**

When you hit vivid imagery, pause and ask:

> What FEELING or MEANING is this image building?

A specific sensory detail almost always carries one of:

- **Mood** — the smell of bread in a warm kitchen builds comfort; the sound of a clock in an empty room builds loneliness.
- **Tone** — "the soft yellow light of the lamp" carries the narrator's tenderness; "the harsh yellow glare of the lamp" carries criticism or unease.
- **Character** — what a character notices reveals what they care about. A grieving character might notice every empty chair.
- **Theme** — recurring imagery (water, light, doors) often connects to the story's larger claim.

**Examples:**

- *"The bread came out warm, the crust crackling like paper."* — sensory detail building comfort, possibly nostalgia.
- *"The hallway smelled of bleach and stale flowers."* — sensory detail building uneasiness, possibly a hospital or funeral scene.
- *"She traced the names on the cold stone with her finger."* — touch detail building reverence or grief.

**Trap to avoid:**

Don't treat imagery as just "the author is describing the scene." Real imagery has a JOB. If you can't say what feeling or meaning the imagery is building, you're missing the point of the passage.

**On a test:**

When a question asks "What is the EFFECT of the imagery in lines X–Y?" — the answer is the feeling, mood, or meaning the imagery creates, not what the imagery literally shows.

### Why It Matters

Imagery questions are a constant on MAP literary reading. The wrong answer is usually "the imagery describes the scene" — true but misses the WHY.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** "The bread came out warm, the crust crackling like paper."
- Answer: Building comfort / nostalgia.
- Explanation: Warm + crackling + the kitchen association creates a comfortable, possibly nostalgic feeling. The imagery is doing emotional work, not just describing.

**Example 2.** "The hallway smelled of bleach and stale flowers."
- Answer: Building uneasiness — likely a hospital or funeral setting.
- Explanation: Bleach is sterile, institutional; stale flowers point to a place where flowers are brought (hospital, funeral home). The imagery is setting mood through association.

**Example 3.** "She traced the names on the cold stone with her finger."
- Answer: Building reverence or grief.
- Explanation: Touch (tracing), temperature (cold), and the names on stone together build a reverent or grieving feeling. The image is doing emotional work.

**Example 4.** "He noticed the empty chair at the head of the table before he noticed anything else."
- Answer: Building grief or absence; revealing what the character cares about.
- Explanation: What a character notices is character revelation. Noticing the empty chair FIRST tells us about the character's state — they're attuned to absence.

## B1.1 — Topic vs Main Idea

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b1/01-topic-vs-main-idea.csv` + `content/quizzes/b1/01-topic-vs-main-idea.csv`
**Subtitle:** Topic = the subject. Main idea = what the article SAYS about it.
**Quick reference:** Don't confuse the topic with the main idea. Topic is what the article is ABOUT. Main idea is what the article CLAIMS or EXPLAINS about that topic.

**Common traps:**
- Picking a topic word when the question asks for the main idea.
- Picking a true detail that doesn't capture the whole article.
- Picking a claim that's bigger than what the article actually argues.

### Mini-Lesson

**Topic = subject. Main idea = what the author says about it.**

A common wrong answer on MAP main-idea questions is a TOPIC word. The student picks "electric cars" or "the Civil War" or "recycling." Those are topics. The main idea is what the article CLAIMS about that subject.

**The single-sentence test:**

If you can finish this sentence, you have a main idea:

> The article is making the claim/explanation that ___.

If you can only fill in a single word or short phrase, you have a topic.

**Examples:**

- *Topic:* electric cars → *Main idea:* electric cars are now competitive with gas cars on price, but charging infrastructure remains the biggest obstacle.
- *Topic:* recycling → *Main idea:* recycling helps less than people think because most plastics aren't actually recycled — reducing use is more effective.
- *Topic:* urban heat islands → *Main idea:* cities can reduce dangerous heat by adding shade, reflective surfaces, and green space.

A main idea has a subject AND a claim.

**Where the main idea usually lives:**

- In a short informational article: usually in the first or last paragraph.
- In a longer article: often stated in the introduction and restated in the conclusion.
- In opinion pieces: often the THESIS — a full sentence the author defends throughout.

If you can't find a sentence that captures the article's claim, build one by combining what each paragraph contributes.

**The test trap:**

Wrong-answer choices include:

- A topic word ("climate change") that's true but too narrow.
- A detail that's true but doesn't capture the whole article.
- A claim that's too sweeping for what the article actually says.

The right answer is the SENTENCE-shaped claim that captures the whole article — no bigger, no smaller.

### Why It Matters

Main-idea questions are constant on MAP informational reading. The "topic instead of main idea" wrong answer is one of the highest-frequency traps.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Electric cars
- Answer: Topic — not main idea.
- Explanation: A subject word. To become a main idea, it needs a claim about what the article is saying about electric cars.

**Example 2.** Electric cars are now competitive with gas cars on price, but charging infrastructure remains the biggest obstacle.
- Answer: Main idea — claim about the topic.
- Explanation: Subject + claim + supported by the article. Main-idea shape.

**Example 3.** Most plastics in the recycling bin are not actually recycled.
- Answer: A SUPPORTING DETAIL, not a main idea.
- Explanation: True and important, but doesn't capture the whole article. The main idea uses this detail in service of a larger claim about recycling's effectiveness.

**Example 4.** Reducing consumption is more effective than recycling for shrinking plastic waste.
- Answer: Main idea.
- Explanation: Captures the article's argument: the comparison between reducing and recycling, with a verdict.

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b1/02-main-idea-vs-detail.csv` + `content/quizzes/b1/02-main-idea-vs-detail.csv`
**Subtitle:** A detail proves the main idea. It isn't the main idea itself.
**Quick reference:** A supporting detail is true and from the text — but it's only one piece of the argument. The main idea is the BIGGER claim the detail supports.

**Common traps:**
- Picking a true detail instead of the main idea (because the detail "felt right" from reading).
- Failing to check whether the answer covers the whole article, or just one paragraph.
- Treating the most specific true statement as the main idea (specific is usually a detail; main idea is broader).

### Mini-Lesson

**A detail is what proves the main idea — not what the main idea IS.**

This is the second-most-common main-idea trap (after "picking a topic"). The student picks an answer that is TRUE in the article, but the truth is only a detail — one piece of evidence — not the article's overall claim.

**The relationship:**

- **Main idea** — the article's overall claim. One sentence that captures the whole.
- **Supporting detail** — a fact, statistic, example, or quote that PROVES the main idea.

**A main idea has many supporting details. A supporting detail has one main idea above it.**

**Example:**

Article: *Honeybee populations have declined since 2006. Pesticides are a major cause. Habitat loss has reduced their food sources. The Varroa mite has spread widely. Researchers say solving this requires addressing all three at once.*

- *Main idea:* Bee decline has multiple causes that must be addressed together.
- *Supporting details:*
  - Pesticides are a major cause.
  - Habitat loss has reduced food sources.
  - Varroa mites have spread.
  - Researchers recommend addressing all three.

Each detail supports the main idea. None of them IS the main idea.

**Test trap to watch for:**

When a question asks for the main idea, the wrong answers will often be TRUE details from the article. They feel right because you saw them in the text. The discrimination move:

> Ask: does this answer capture the WHOLE article, or just one piece of it?

If it captures only one piece, it's a detail. If it captures the whole, it's the main idea.

**Two-part check:**

1. Is the answer TRUE in the article? (If not, eliminate.)
2. Does it capture the WHOLE article, or just part? (If just part, it's a detail.)

### Why It Matters

Detail-vs-main-idea is the trap students fall into when they remember reading something true and pick that as the main idea. Tests reward students who check whether the answer covers the whole article.

### Worked Examples

- Answer: Supporting detail, not main idea.
- Explanation: True and in the article. But it's only ONE of the article's causes — it doesn't capture the article's overall claim about addressing causes together.

**Example 2.** Same article. Statement: "Researchers say no single fix will solve the bee decline."

**Example 3.** Article main idea: "Returning to the moon is now a political and economic challenge, not a technological one." Statement: "Apollo rockets could be rebuilt today."

- Explanation: True — and important. It supports the main idea ("the ability still exists") but doesn't capture the article's whole claim about why we haven't returned.

**Example 4.** Article main idea: "Public libraries are becoming community centers, not just book repositories." Statement: "Libraries provide internet access."

- Explanation: True. One of several services the article lists. The main idea names the PATTERN; the detail is one example.

## B2.1 — Evidence Must Be Traceable to the Text

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b2/01-evidence-traceable.csv` + `content/quizzes/b2/01-evidence-traceable.csv`
**Subtitle:** You can explain in your own words, but you must be able to point to the exact words that prove it.
**Quick reference:** Evidence is the SPECIFIC WORDS in the text that prove your answer. You can paraphrase to explain, but you must be able to point back to the exact text.

**Common traps:**
- Summarizing the right idea without being able to point to specific words in the text.
- Confusing vague gesture ("it talks about that") with locating evidence.
- Picking a paraphrase when the question asks for the exact words from the passage.

### Mini-Lesson

**Evidence is locatable.**

A common test failure: a student writes an answer that is correct in spirit but can't point to the words in the text that prove it. On evidence questions, that fails.

**The rule:**

You can explain in your own words, but you must be able to point to the **exact words in the text** that prove your answer.

**Two valid moves:**

1. **Quote** — copy the relevant words directly.
2. **Locate** — say which paragraph (or sentence) contains the proof, and what specific phrase carries it.

What's NOT valid: vague gestures like "it says somewhere" or paraphrases with no anchor.

**Why this skill matters on tests:**

MAP and similar tests often ask:

- *"Which sentence best supports the answer?"*
- *"Which part of the passage proves...?"*
- *"What evidence in the passage shows...?"*

For these questions, the right answer is a specific phrase or sentence from the text. Paraphrases — even correct ones — usually aren't the right answer.

**The discipline:**

Whenever you make a claim about a passage, ask:

> What exact words in the text could I point to as proof?

If the answer is "I just know," you don't have evidence yet. Find the words.

**Common trap:**

A wrong-answer choice will be TRUE about the passage but won't be the BEST evidence. Look for the answer that is most directly supportive — the most specific, most locatable phrase.

### Why It Matters

Evidence-traceability is foundational for advanced reading. Plateaued students often summarize the right answer but can't point to the proof — and lose points for it.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Question: Which words from the article prove that the author thinks the policy worked? Student answer: "The author thinks the policy is good."
- Answer: Not evidence — a paraphrase without text anchor.
- Explanation: The student's claim might be correct, but evidence requires pointing to the SPECIFIC WORDS that prove it. Without a quote or locatable phrase, this isn't evidence.

**Example 2.** Same question. Student answer: "In paragraph 3, the author writes 'the new rule has produced clear gains.'"
- Answer: Strong evidence — direct quote with location.
- Explanation: Locates the paragraph AND quotes the specific phrase. Anyone reading the passage can verify this exact wording supports the claim.

**Example 3.** Question: Which detail supports the inference that the company is hiding financial trouble? Student answer: "The company is struggling."
- Answer: Not evidence — a paraphrase, not a locatable detail.
- Explanation: A summary isn't evidence. The student needs to point to a specific text detail (e.g., "they announced layoffs in the third quarter") that supports the inference.

**Example 4.** Same question. Student answer: "The text mentions the layoffs in paragraph 4 and the missed earnings target in paragraph 5."
- Answer: Strong evidence — multiple specific details, each locatable.
- Explanation: Two specific details, both locatable. Each supports the inference. Multiple traceable details make the answer stronger.

## B2.3 — Proof vs Related Information

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b2/03-proof-vs-related.csv` + `content/quizzes/b2/03-proof-vs-related.csv`
**Subtitle:** Related-but-not-proven is the #1 advanced-reader trap.
**Quick reference:** An answer can be related to the topic and even true, yet still NOT prove the specific claim. Look for the answer that actually proves the question's claim — not just one that's on the same topic.

**Common traps:**
- Picking an answer that's TRUE but doesn't prove the specific claim — the related-but-not-proven trap.
- Failing to check whether the answer directly addresses the question's CLAIM, not just its TOPIC.
- Getting caught between two true answers without using the proof-specificity test.

### Mini-Lesson

**Related ≠ proven.**

The single most common advanced-reader trap on MAP and similar tests is this: wrong answers that **sound connected** to the question but don't actually **prove** the claim being asked about.

**Example:**

Article: *Many U.S. cities have invested in bike lanes over the past decade. In cities that added more than 100 miles of protected bike lanes, both bicycle commuting and bicycle safety improved.*

Question: *Which sentence best supports the claim that protected bike lanes improve bicycle safety?*

Wrong-but-tempting: *"Many U.S. cities have invested in bike lanes over the past decade."*

That's RELATED. It's about bike lanes. But it doesn't prove the SAFETY claim. The right answer is the specific sentence about safety improving in cities with protected lanes.

**The test:**

For each answer choice, ask:

> Does this answer specifically prove the claim the question is asking about, or is it just on the same topic?

**Related answers feel right because:**

- They're true (in the text).
- They're on the right topic.
- The reader recognizes them.

But they don't satisfy the specific question. The discipline is to ask: *does this answer prove the EXACT claim?*

**Two-step discrimination:**

1. Eliminate any answer that's false or outside the text.
2. Of the remaining true answers, pick the one that DIRECTLY proves the question's specific claim — not the ones that are just on-topic.

**This is the highest-leverage skill for breaking through the 240–250 plateau.** Plateaued readers can usually eliminate obvious wrong answers but get caught between two TRUE answers — one that proves, one that's just related.

### Why It Matters

Proof-vs-related is the #1 trap on advanced evidence questions. Mastering this single skill is one of the highest-leverage moves for breaking 250.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Question: Which sentence best supports the claim that protected bike lanes improve SAFETY? Wrong-tempting answer: "Many U.S. cities have invested in bike lanes."
- Answer: Related but doesn't prove. The claim is about safety; this answer is about investment.
- Explanation: True and on the right topic. But the question asks for proof of SAFETY, and this sentence doesn't address safety. Related ≠ proven.

**Example 2.** Same question. Better answer: "In cities that added more than 100 miles of protected bike lanes, bicycle safety improved."
- Answer: Direct proof. Names protected bike lanes AND links them to safety improving.
- Explanation: This is the exact claim the question asks about. Topic match + claim match = proof.

**Example 3.** Question: Which detail best supports that the new policy increased school attendance? Wrong-tempting: "The new policy was introduced last September."
- Answer: Related but doesn't prove.
- Explanation: True but only about WHEN the policy started, not whether it worked. The question is about attendance — this answer doesn't address it.

**Example 4.** Same question. Better answer: "School attendance rose 12 percent in the year after the new policy took effect."
- Answer: Direct proof.
- Explanation: Names attendance AND links it to the policy. The specific claim the question asks about.

## B2.5 — Inference from Nonfiction Cues

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b2/05-inference-from-nonfiction.csv` + `content/quizzes/b2/05-inference-from-nonfiction.csv`
**Subtitle:** Drawing conclusions from data, claims, and stated facts in informational text.
**Quick reference:** Nonfiction inference uses different clues than literary inference. Look at the data, the claims, the structure, and what the author chose to emphasize — not character actions or dialogue.

**Common traps:**
- Treating nonfiction inference like literary inference — looking for character clues when the clues are data.
- Over-inferring from data — assuming causation when the data only supports correlation.
- Missing the importance of what the author CHOSE not to say — omissions can be inference clues too.

### Mini-Lesson

**Nonfiction inference is a distinct skill.**

In literary text, inference uses clues like character actions, dialogue, scene details. In nonfiction, the clues are different:

- **Stated facts and data** — what the numbers add up to that the author doesn't say outright.
- **The author's choices** — what was emphasized, what was downplayed, what was included or omitted.
- **Word choice** — hedging verbs ("suggests," "claims") vs. demonstration verbs ("proves," "shows").
- **Structure** — what the author put first, what was buried, what was used as a counterpoint.
- **What's missing** — sometimes the strongest inference comes from what the author DIDN'T say. Omissions support only tentative inferences when the text gives a reason the missing information should have appeared.

**Example:**

Article: *The new policy was adopted in 2018. By 2023, complaint volume had risen 40 percent. The policy's authors have so far declined to comment.*

Stated facts: policy in 2018, complaints up 40%, authors decline to comment.

Reasonable inferences:
- The policy may not be working as intended (rising complaints suggest a problem).
- The authors may be unwilling to defend the policy publicly (declining comment is unusual for a successful policy).

These aren't STATED in the article. They're INFERRED from the cues — the data trend plus the absence of comment.

**Where students go wrong:**

1. **Treating nonfiction inference like literary inference** — looking for character actions when the clues are data, structure, and word choice.
2. **Over-inferring** — same trap as literary, but worse because data feels solid. A 40% rise in complaints might suggest the policy is failing — or it might suggest people are more aware of it.
3. **Speculation** — guessing causes the data doesn't support.

**The rule:**

The smallest claim the data and stated facts support is usually the right inference. Stay close to what the text actually shows.

### Why It Matters

Nonfiction inference is a MAP-tested skill that's distinct from literary inference. Plateaued readers often miss this when the question demands inference from FACTS instead of from CHARACTER.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Article: "The new policy was adopted in 2018. By 2023, complaints had risen 40%. The policy's authors have declined to comment." Inference: The policy may not be working as intended; the authors may be unwilling to defend it publicly.
- Answer: Reasonable nonfiction inference.
- Explanation: Two clues — the data trend AND the silence — combine for an inference the text doesn't state outright. Small, defensible.

**Example 2.** Same article. Inference: The policy is a complete failure that has ruined people's lives.
- Answer: Over-inference.
- Explanation: The data supports SOME problem (rising complaints, no defense). It doesn't support "complete failure" or "ruined lives." Stay close to what's actually there.

**Example 3.** Article: "A study compared two groups of students. Group A studied with music. Group B studied in silence. Group B scored higher on the test." Inference: Music while studying may distract from learning.
- Answer: Reasonable inference.
- Explanation: The data SUPPORTS this small claim. Note "may" — that's the right hedge. Going further ("music ruins learning") would over-infer.

**Example 4.** Article: "In her 30-page report on the program, the director devoted 28 pages to its successes and 2 pages to its challenges." Inference: The report may be presenting the program more favorably than fully balanced.
- Answer: Inference from STRUCTURE — what the author chose to emphasize.
- Explanation: The structural cue (28 vs 2 pages) supports an inference about emphasis. The reader is reading the author's choices, not just the content.

## B4.1 — Neutral vs Skeptical

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b4/01-neutral-vs-skeptical.csv` + `content/quizzes/b4/01-neutral-vs-skeptical.csv`
**Subtitle:** The most-missed tone distinction at advanced levels.
**Quick reference:** Words like "claimed," "supposedly," or "although" can signal doubt. Test them in context before calling the tone skeptical.

**Common traps:**
- Students see facts plus calm language and default to "neutral," missing the diction that signals doubt.
- Students treat hedging verbs ("claimed," "alleged") as synonyms for plain reporting verbs ("said," "announced").
- Students assume openly negative words are required for skepticism — but most skeptical writing hides its doubt in word choice.

### Mini-Lesson

**Neutral** and **skeptical** look similar on the page. Both can describe factual content. Both can sound calm. But they're not the same — and confusing them is the most common tone mistake at the Break-250 level.

A **neutral** author reports without leaning. They use plain reporting verbs ("said," "announced," "reported"). They give facts and let the reader judge.

A **skeptical** author looks like they're reporting facts — but their word choice quietly signals doubt. They use:

- **Reporting verbs that hedge:** "claimed," "alleged," "asserted," "supposedly," "reportedly"
- **Contrast words:** "although," "however," "despite," "yet"
- **Distancing phrases:** "so-called," "what they call," "if true," "according to"

The doubt is real, but it's hidden in the word choice rather than stated directly.

**How to use this tool:**

1. Scan for hedging verbs (claimed, supposedly, allegedly, reportedly).
2. Scan for contrast words (although, however, despite, yet).
3. Scan for distancing phrases (so-called, what they call, if true).
4. Treat these as signals to test. The tone is **skeptical** only when they distance, hedge, or undermine a claim in context.

If none of these signals appear and the language is plain, the tone is genuinely **neutral**.

### Why It Matters

Plateaued readers see factual content and calm sentences, then default to "neutral." But word choice is the giveaway. Authors of skeptical writing rarely use openly negative words — they hide their doubt in hedging verbs and contrast words. Catching those signals is one of the highest-leverage moves at the 240 → 250 jump.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** The company claimed its new bottle was eco-friendly, although it used nearly the same amount of plastic as before.
- Answer: Skeptical
- Explanation: "Claimed" (instead of "said") and "although" (signaling contrast) both undermine the company's statement. The author isn't reporting neutrally — they're hedging.

**Example 2.** The city council voted 7-2 to approve the budget. The new spending plan goes into effect July 1.
- Answer: Neutral
- Explanation: Plain reporting ("voted," "goes into effect"); no hedging, no contrast words. The author is presenting facts without signaling attitude.

**Example 3.** Supporters argue the policy will reduce traffic; however, early data shows little change in commute times.
- Answer: Skeptical
- Explanation: "Argue" hedges (compare to "showed"); "however" introduces evidence that undermines the claim. Quietly skeptical of the supporters.

**Example 4.** The new bridge opens Friday after three years of construction. Officials say it will serve 50,000 commuters daily.
- Answer: Neutral
- Explanation: "Officials say" is direct attribution. No hedging verbs, no contrast words. The author is reporting plainly.

## B5.3 — Paragraph Function

**File pair:** `content/lessons/b5/03-paragraph-function.csv` + `content/quizzes/b5/03-paragraph-function.csv`
**Subtitle:** Every paragraph has a JOB. Read for the job, not just the content.
**Quick reference:** Each paragraph in nonfiction has a job — introduce, define, give an example, provide evidence, contrast, counter, refute, conclude. Read for the job, not just the content.

**Common traps:**
- Summarizing a paragraph's CONTENT instead of naming its JOB.
- Thinking every paragraph "explains" or "talks about" something — too generic to be a real function.
- Missing that one paragraph can do MULTIPLE jobs (introduce + define, contrast + counter).

### Mini-Lesson

**Paragraphs aren't just chunks of text. They have JOBS.**

In informational writing, each paragraph usually does one specific kind of work. Advanced readers learn to recognize the job — and the job often answers the test question.

**Common paragraph functions:**

- **Introduce** — set up the topic, often with a hook or thesis.
- **Provide background** — give context the reader needs.
- **Define a term** — explain a concept the rest of the article uses.
- **Give an example** — illustrate a claim with a specific case.
- **Provide evidence** — present data, study results, or specific facts.
- **Show contrast** — compare two things or show a counterpoint.
- **Present a counterargument** — give the opposing view (often to refute it).
- **Refute** — argue against a position.
- **Explain cause / effect** — show how one thing led to another.
- **Conclude** — restate the main idea or call for action.

**The reading move:**

When you finish a paragraph, ask:

> What JOB did that paragraph just do?

If you can name the job, you understand the paragraph's role. If you can't, you've probably read the words without understanding the structure.

**On a test:**

Questions like *"What is the role of paragraph 3?"* or *"Why does the author include paragraph 4?"* are paragraph-function questions. The answer is one of the verbs above (illustrate, refute, define, etc.) — not a summary of the paragraph's content.

**Common trap:**

A wrong-answer choice will SUMMARIZE the paragraph's content rather than name its job. Both might be true, but the job is what the question is asking for.

- Wrong (content summary): "Paragraph 3 discusses the new policy."
- Right (function): "Paragraph 3 provides evidence that the new policy is working."

The second names what the paragraph DOES, not just what it talks about.

### Why It Matters

Paragraph-function questions are constant on MAP informational reading. Students who can name the function (instead of just summarizing content) score significantly higher.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Paragraph 1 of an article: "Honeybee populations have declined since 2006. Researchers say the causes are complex and the solutions are not obvious."
- Answer: Function: Introduce — sets up the topic and signals the article's complexity.
- Explanation: Opens the article with the topic (decline) and a hook (complex causes). Classic introduce function.

**Example 2.** Paragraph 4: "Consider the case of Vermont, where pesticide restrictions and habitat replanting together produced a 30 percent rebound in honeybee populations between 2018 and 2022."
- Answer: Function: Give an example — illustrates the article's claim with a specific case.
- Explanation: "Consider the case of X" is a classic example-paragraph opener. The Vermont specifics illustrate the broader claim.

**Example 3.** Paragraph 5: "Some critics argue that the multi-cause framing lets pesticide manufacturers avoid full responsibility."
- Answer: Function: Present a counterargument.
- Explanation: "Some critics argue..." introduces opposing view. The paragraph's job is to present that view, often before the article addresses it.

**Example 4.** Final paragraph: "What the bee research makes clear is that simple problems often have causes we haven't yet learned to see — and that patient, systemic responses tend to work better than dramatic single fixes."
- Answer: Function: Conclude — restates and extends the main idea.
- Explanation: Final paragraph that synthesizes ("what the research makes clear") and generalizes ("systemic responses tend to work better"). Classic conclude function.

- A. Define a term.
- B. Present a counterargument — the paragraph gives the opposing view, often before the article addresses it. **[correct]**
- C. Conclude.
- D. Provide background.

Feedback:
- A: No definition.
- B: Correct. "Some critics argue" is a classic counterargument introduction. The paragraph's job is to give voice to the opposing view.
- C: Doesn't conclude — opens a debate.
- D: Not background — argumentative content.

#### Q5 (medium)

A paragraph reads: "What the research makes clear is that simple problems often have causes we haven't yet learned to see — and that patient, systemic responses tend to work better than dramatic single fixes." Most likely FUNCTION?

- A. Introduce.
- B. Give an example.
- C. Conclude — synthesizes the article's findings and extends them into a generalization. **[correct]**
- D. Refute.

Feedback:
- A: Doesn't introduce — too synthetic for an opener.
- B: No specific example — generalization.
- C: Correct. "What the research makes clear" + the generalization at the end = classic conclude function.
- D: No refutation — synthesis.

#### Q6 (medium)

A student answers a paragraph-function question with "Paragraph 3 talks about the new policy." What's wrong with this answer?
